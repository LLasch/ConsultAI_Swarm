# Case Brief: United Parcel Service — IPO Valuation (1999)

## Client Overview
United Parcel Service (UPS) was the largest parcel delivery company in the world in 1999. Founded in 1907 by 19-year-old James Casey with $100 borrowed from a friend, UPS had grown over 92 years into a private, employee-owned operation with 340,000 employees, 149,000 delivery vehicles, 500 planes, and $25 billion in annual revenue. The company delivered nearly 13 million packages daily to over 200 countries. In 1999, Fortune named UPS the "World's Most Admired Global Mail, Package and Freight Delivery Company" and Forbes named it "Company of the Year."

## Current Situation
In July 1999, UPS announced it would end more than 90 years as a private company and launch an initial public offering — expected to be the largest IPO in U.S. history. The central challenge was determining an appropriate price for the new shares, which required evaluating UPS's position in the package delivery industry, its competitive advantages relative to FedEx and USPS, its financial performance, and appropriate valuation benchmarks.

The IPO was not driven by a need to raise capital — UPS had $1.7 billion in net income and a AAA credit rating. Instead, the primary benefit was obtaining publicly traded stock as a tax-efficient medium of exchange to fund strategic acquisitions in a globalizing, consolidating industry.

## Industry Context
The U.S. package delivery market was a $43 billion industry in 1999, segmented into air (overnight + deferred, 60% of revenue) and ground (40% of revenue). Three players dominated: UPS (51% market share by revenue), FedEx (26%), and USPS (17%).

Key industry dynamics:
- Air express had grown at double-digit rates in the 1980s and high single digits in the 1990s
- Ground grew somewhat above GDP rate
- E-commerce was emerging as a potential demand driver (B2C online sales projected to exceed $100B by 2003)
- Document digitization and email threatened the overnight letter segment
- International delivery was the next growth frontier as the U.S. market saturated
- The industry was capital-intensive with high barriers to entry

## Key Challenges / Strategic Questions
1. **Valuation methodology**: Should UPS be valued using FedEx multiples (the direct competitor approach) or "best of breed" multiples from dominant companies in other industries (Coca-Cola, Wal-Mart, Home Depot)?
2. **IPO pricing**: How to balance leaving money on the table (pricing too low) vs. a poor first-day performance (pricing too high)?
3. **Preserving culture**: How to maintain the employee-ownership culture and operational discipline that made UPS successful after going public?
4. **Growth strategy**: How to fund expansion into globalization, e-commerce logistics, and supply chain management?
5. **Competitive positioning**: How to defend against FedEx's ground expansion (via RPS acquisition) and USPS's increasing aggression in parcel delivery?

## Stakeholder Perspectives

**James Kelly, UPS Chairman & CEO (career UPS employee, started as package driver):**
"This is an historic step for UPS. We intend to remain the pre-eminent company in our industry and expand our role as an enabler of global commerce. A publicly traded stock will build on our financial strength as a triple-A rated company and give us more flexibility to pursue strategic opportunities around the world."

**UPS Management's position on valuation:**
Management argued UPS should be valued at a "best of breed" premium, comparing to dominant industry leaders like Coca-Cola (P/E of 39 vs. PepsiCo's 25), Wal-Mart (P/E of 46 vs. Target's 27), and Home Depot (P/E of 51 vs. Lowe's 34). UPS's financial performance supported this — 6.5% net margins and 25.2% ROE vs. FedEx's 2.8% margins and 10.6% ROE.

**Morgan Stanley Deal Team's position:**
The investment bankers favored using FedEx as the primary benchmark. FedEx traded at a P/E of 19.8, price/revenue of 0.74, and market/book of 2.68. The question was whether UPS deserved a premium, and how large.

**FedEx Chairman Fred Smith:**
Positioned FedEx's RPS acquisition as transforming the company into a "global transportation and logistics powerhouse" — signaling aggressive competition with UPS on ground delivery using cheaper contracted (non-union) drivers.

**Employee-owners (two-thirds of equity):**
The board had been setting the stock price at $25.50/share for internal buybacks. The IPO would provide liquidity — employees could divest up to 10% of holdings initially, with gradual market access over the following 18 months. The dual-class share structure (Class A: 10 votes, Class B for public: 1 vote) ensured employees retained ~99% of voting control.

## Financial Highlights

### UPS Performance (1994-1998)
- Revenue: $19.6B (1994) → $24.8B (1998), CAGR ~6%
- Net Income: $943M (1994) → $1,741M (1998) — 84% increase over 4 years
- Net Margin: 4.8% (1994) → 7.0% (1998)
- ROE: averaged 25.2% (1997-1999)
- Total Assets: $11.2B (1994) → $17.1B (1998)
- Shareholders' Equity: $4.6B (1994) → $7.2B (1998)
- Annual CapEx: $1.6B-$2.3B per year
- AAA credit rating
- 1997 Teamsters strike: cost several hundred million in lost revenue, temporarily depressed that year's results

### UPS 1999 Tax Issue
A major complication: in Q2 1999, UPS recorded a $1.442 billion after-tax charge from a U.S. Tax Court ruling regarding Overseas Partners Ltd. (a Bermuda reinsurance entity). Total exposure could reach $2.353B. UPS deposited $1.349B with the IRS and was appealing. This one-time charge reduced reported 1999 net income significantly, but the underlying business earned an estimated $2.325B (excluding the charge).

### FedEx Comparison (FY1999, ending May 31)
- Revenue: $16.8B
- Net Income: $631M
- Net Margin: 3.8%
- ROE: 10.6% (3-year average)
- Total Assets: $10.6B
- Market Cap: ~$12.5B ($41.50/share × 300.6M shares)
- P/E: 19.8
- Price/Revenue: 0.74
- Market/Book: 2.68
- BBB credit rating (vs. UPS's AAA)
- Equity Beta: 1.16

### Key Financial Differences
- UPS had 2.3x FedEx's net margins and 2.4x its ROE
- UPS operated an integrated ground+air network (one fleet of trucks, shared sorting centers); FedEx ran separate operating units
- FedEx used operating leases extensively for aircraft ($13.1B in total future operating lease payments); UPS used capital leases and owned more of its fleet ($1.1B in operating lease obligations)
- FedEx used cheaper contracted drivers; UPS had Teamster-unionized workforce (highest paid in industry, but also most loyal — <5% annual turnover)

## UPS Operating Advantages
1. **Integrated network**: One fleet handles both ground and air — if a "Next Day Air" package can get there by truck faster/cheaper, the system routes it by ground. FedEx runs separate systems.
2. **Volume density**: 12.4M packages/day vs. FedEx's 4.5M. UPS drivers pick up and deliver significantly more packages per hour.
3. **Technology**: $1B+/year in IT investment. Could handle 6x more online tracking requests than FedEx. Forbes called UPS "a technology company with trucks."
4. **Employee culture**: Promote-from-within policy, low turnover, institutional knowledge. CEO started as a driver.
5. **Ground dominance**: 80% ground market share — the economic moat of the business.

## UPS Strategic Growth Vectors (1999)
1. **Globalization**: International trade was 25%+ of U.S. GDP (up from 11% in 1970). UPS had 37,000 non-U.S. employees delivering 1M packages/day to 200+ countries.
2. **E-Commerce**: UPS was already the preferred shipper for online commerce, with functionality on 10,000+ business websites. B2C online sales projected to exceed $100B by 2003.
3. **Supply Chain Management**: UPS Logistics Group (formed 1993) generated ~$1B in revenue by 1999. Contracts with Sprint PCS, Papa John's, Ford.

## Constraints
- Dual-class share structure limits public investor voting power
- Teamsters union: 200,000+ members, highest-paid workforce in industry, risk of future work stoppages (1997 strike precedent)
- $2.35B total tax exposure from OPL ruling — appeals process could take years
- International expansion lagged FedEx (UPS didn't yet have China flight rights)
- Culture preservation: going public introduces quarterly earnings pressure on a company that historically thought in decades

## Valuation Benchmarks

### FedEx Multiples (Direct Competitor)
- P/E: 19.8
- Price/Revenue: 0.74
- Market/Book: 2.68

### "Best of Breed" Multiples (Industry Leaders)
| Company | P/E | Market/Book | ROE |
|---------|-----|-------------|-----|
| Home Depot | 50.9 | 12.7 | 25% |
| Lowe's | 34.1 | 5.8 | 17% |
| Coca-Cola | 39.3 | 15.6 | 40% |
| PepsiCo | 25.5 | 7.9 | 31% |
| Wal-Mart | 46.1 | 11.6 | 25% |
| Target | 27.0 | 6.0 | 22% |

The "best of breed" leaders commanded P/E premiums of 45-70% over their respective #2 competitors.

### Lease Adjustments
FedEx's heavy use of operating leases ($13.1B in future obligations) means its balance sheet understates its true asset base and debt. Capitalizing these leases would increase FedEx's effective debt, lower its equity ratio, and make its valuation multiples less directly comparable to UPS's.
