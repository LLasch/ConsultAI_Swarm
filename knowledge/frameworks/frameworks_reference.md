# Consulting Frameworks — Applied Reference Guide


## Porter's Five Forces

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: Porter's Five Forces (Michael Porter, Harvard Business School, 1979)

PURPOSE: Analyze the competitive intensity and attractiveness of an industry.

THE FIVE FORCES:
1. Threat of New Entrants — How easy is it for new competitors to enter? Consider: capital requirements, economies of scale, brand loyalty, regulatory barriers, access to distribution, switching costs.
2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers — Can suppliers raise prices or reduce quality? Consider: number of suppliers, uniqueness of product, switching costs, supplier concentration, threat of forward integration.
3. Bargaining Power of Buyers — Can customers push prices down? Consider: number of buyers, volume per buyer, switching costs, price sensitivity, availability of substitutes, buyer information.
4. Threat of Substitutes — Can customers meet the same need a different way? NOTE: Substitutes are NOT competitors — they're entirely different products that serve the same need (email is a substitute for overnight letters; FedEx is a competitor to UPS).
5. Rivalry Among Existing Competitors — How intensely do current players compete? Consider: number of competitors, industry growth rate, fixed costs, differentiation, exit barriers.

HOW TO APPLY: Rate each force Low / Medium / High. The more forces that are High, the less attractive the industry. The insight is in the "why," not the rating. Take a clear position — rating everything "medium" is useless.

WORKED EXAMPLE — U.S. Package Delivery (UPS IPO case):
- New Entrants: LOW — $10B+ for a national network, decades to build route density. Only Amazon has entered in 25 years.
- Supplier Power: MEDIUM — Limited substitutes for jet fuel, but UPS's scale gives negotiating leverage.
- Buyer Power: MEDIUM-HIGH — Large corporate customers (Amazon, Walmart) can negotiate aggressively.
- Substitutes: MEDIUM — Email replaced overnight letters; physical goods still need physical delivery.
- Rivalry: HIGH — Three dominant players (UPS, FedEx, USPS) with high fixed costs competing intensely.
- CONCLUSION: Moderately attractive industry — high barriers protect incumbents, but rivalry and buyer power compress margins.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Confusing competitors with substitutes
- Treating it as static instead of analyzing how forces are changing over time
- Rating all forces as "medium" — take a position
- Adding "government" as a sixth force — regulation affects all five, it is not a separate force



## SWOT Analysis with SO/WO/ST/WT Matrix

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: SWOT Analysis with SO/WO/ST/WT Cross-Reference Matrix

PURPOSE: Organize internal Strengths and Weaknesses alongside external Opportunities and Threats, then cross-reference to generate strategic options.

THE FOUR QUADRANTS:
- Strengths (Internal, Helpful): What the organization does well
- Weaknesses (Internal, Harmful): Internal gaps or limitations
- Opportunities (External, Helpful): Market trends or gaps to exploit
- Threats (External, Harmful): External forces that could harm performance

THE REAL VALUE — SO/WO/ST/WT CROSS-REFERENCE:
- SO Strategies (Strength + Opportunity): Use strengths to capture opportunities. Your GROWTH PLAY.
- WO Strategies (Weakness + Opportunity): Fix weaknesses to unlock opportunities. Your INVESTMENT PLAY.
- ST Strategies (Strength + Threat): Use strengths to defend against threats. Your DEFENSIVE PLAY.
- WT Strategies (Weakness + Threat): Minimize weaknesses and avoid threats. Your SURVIVAL PLAY.

WORKED EXAMPLE — YMCA Membership:
Strengths: Only pool in the area, strong community brand, 92% childcare utilization
Weaknesses: Flat pricing model, no corporate product, aging facility, no sales team
Opportunities: 3 business parks within 5km (~8,000 employees), corporate wellness budgets growing post-COVID
Threats: GoodLife Fitness 2km away, home fitness trend, 18 months of reserves remaining

SO Strategy: Pool (strength) + corporate wellness demand (opportunity) = Launch corporate membership with pool access as differentiator GoodLife can't match
WO Strategy: Fix flat pricing (weakness) + off-peak underutilization (opportunity) = Tiered pricing with cheaper off-peak basic tier
WT Strategy: Address declining membership (weakness) + GoodLife competition (threat) = Double down on what GoodLife doesn't have: pool, childcare, community programs

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Listing 20+ items per quadrant — focus on the 3-5 most impactful
- Confusing internal and external (competition is a THREAT, not a weakness)
- Making it a list exercise instead of a strategy tool — cross-referencing is where the value is
- Being vague: "good brand" is not useful. "95% brand recognition among Riverdale families" is.



## BCG Growth-Share Matrix

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: BCG Growth-Share Matrix (Boston Consulting Group, 1970)

PURPOSE: Evaluate business units or products based on market growth rate and relative market share to guide capital allocation.

THE FOUR QUADRANTS:
- STARS (High Growth, High Share): Market leaders in fast-growing markets. Require heavy investment to maintain position. Goal: invest to maintain leadership.
- CASH COWS (Low Growth, High Share): Market leaders in mature markets. Generate more cash than they need. Goal: harvest cash, invest minimally, use profits to fund Stars and Question Marks.
- QUESTION MARKS (High Growth, Low Share): Small players in fast-growing markets. Could become Stars or Dogs. Goal: invest selectively in the ones with the best chance of gaining share.
- DOGS (Low Growth, Low Share): Weak position in stagnant markets. Often break even or lose money. Goal: divest, wind down, or reposition.

WORKED EXAMPLE — FedEx Portfolio (circa 1999):
- Star: FedEx Express (Overnight) — market leader in growing segment, needs continued aircraft investment
- Question Mark: FedEx Ground — high-growth ground market, but only 10% share vs. UPS's 80%. Heavy investment needed.
- Cash Cow: FedEx Freight — mature LTL market, stable returns
- Dog: FedEx Custom Critical — small niche, limited growth
Strategic Implication: Harvest cash from Freight, keep investing in Express, go all-in on Ground. FedEx did exactly this — and it worked.

APPLYING TO NONPROFITS — Programs as Portfolio:
Each program is a "business unit." High growth = rising community need or grant availability. High share = your organization's reach and impact vs. alternatives.
- Star programs: growing need, you're the dominant provider. Invest.
- Cash Cow programs: stable, mature need, you're well-established. Harvest to fund Stars.
- Question Mark programs: growing need but you're not yet the dominant provider. Decide: invest or exit.
- Dog programs: declining need or you're a marginal provider. Reallocate resources.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Using absolute market share instead of relative (relative to the largest competitor)
- Assuming Question Marks automatically become Stars with investment — many become Dogs
- Ignoring that Dogs might still serve a strategic blocking or bundling purpose
- Applying it to single-product companies (the framework is for portfolios)



## Value Chain Analysis

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: Value Chain Analysis (Michael Porter, 1985)

PURPOSE: Identify where within a company's operations value is created and where costs are incurred. Find the activities where competitive advantage is built or lost.

PRIMARY ACTIVITIES:
1. Inbound Logistics — Receiving, storing, and distributing inputs (raw materials, components)
2. Operations — Transforming inputs into the final product or service
3. Outbound Logistics — Distributing the finished product to customers
4. Marketing & Sales — Creating awareness, persuading buyers, pricing
5. Service — Post-sale support, maintenance, returns

SUPPORT ACTIVITIES:
6. Firm Infrastructure — Finance, legal, general management, planning
7. Human Resource Management — Recruiting, training, compensation
8. Technology Development — R&D, process automation, IT systems
9. Procurement — Purchasing inputs

HOW TO APPLY: Map each activity for your company AND your key competitor. Identify where the performance gap is largest — that's where the strategic advantage (or disadvantage) lives.

WORKED EXAMPLE — Zara vs. H&M:
- Design: Zara has 700 in-house designers doing constant trend monitoring (advantage: speed). H&M uses seasonal collections.
- Inbound Logistics: Zara sources 60%+ from nearby Europe. H&M sources from Asia. Zara advantage: speed. H&M advantage: cost.
- Operations: Zara uses small batch production in 2-week cycles. H&M uses large batch with 6-month cycles. Zara advantage: responsiveness.
- Marketing: Zara spends <0.3% of revenue on advertising. H&M spends 3-4%. Zara's rotating inventory IS the marketing.
Key Insight: Zara's entire advantage is concentrated in Design → Operations → Outbound Logistics, all optimized for speed. H&M optimizes for cost. These are fundamentally different strategies.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Listing activities without analyzing them — identify WHERE the advantage lies
- Ignoring linkages between activities (Zara's design speed only works because operations can respond in 2 weeks)
- Treating all activities as equally important — in most businesses, 2-3 activities drive 80% of competitive advantage



## Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: Jobs to Be Done (Clayton Christensen, Harvard Business School)

CORE PRINCIPLE: "People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole." Customers "hire" products and services to get a job done. When you understand the job, you can design a better solution — even if it looks nothing like the current product category.

THE FOUR DIMENSIONS OF A JOB:
1. Functional Job — What practical task are they trying to accomplish?
2. Emotional Job — How do they want to feel during and after?
3. Social Job — How do they want to be perceived by others?
4. Context — When and where does this job arise? What constraints exist?

HOW TO APPLY: For any customer interaction, identify all four dimensions. The functional job is obvious — the emotional and social jobs are where differentiation lives.

WORKED EXAMPLE — Airbnb vs. Hotels:
- Functional: "I need a place to sleep in another city" — both satisfy this
- Functional: "I need a kitchen to feed my family affordably" — Airbnb yes, hotel no
- Emotional: "I want to feel like a local, not a tourist" — Airbnb yes, Marriott no
- Social: "I want interesting travel stories to tell friends" — Airbnb yes ("I stayed in a treehouse!"), hotel no
- Context: Family of 4 for a week on a budget — Airbnb $150/night for whole apartment vs. $300/night for two hotel rooms
Key Insight: Airbnb doesn't just compete with hotels on price — it serves different emotional and social jobs. This is why Airbnb can coexist with hotels rather than fully replacing them.

IMPORTANT APPLICATION — Non-Obvious Competitors:
If the job is "reward myself during my morning commute," the competitors aren't just other coffee shops. They're any product someone might "hire" for that job: a podcast, a gym visit, a nice breakfast at home. Understanding the job reveals who you're actually competing against.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Defining the job too narrowly (the job isn't "buy coffee" — it's "get energy + a small reward during my morning commute")
- Ignoring emotional and social dimensions — these often drive decisions more than functional needs
- Assuming all customers hire the product for the same job (a milkshake bought at 7am serves a different job than one at 5pm for a child)



## Ansoff Matrix — Growth Strategy

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: Ansoff Matrix (Igor Ansoff, 1957)

PURPOSE: Identify growth strategies based on whether a company is targeting existing or new markets with existing or new products.

THE FOUR STRATEGIES (in order of increasing risk):
1. MARKET PENETRATION (Existing Products + Existing Markets) — Sell more of what you have to who you already serve. Lowest risk. Example: Starbucks opening more stores in existing cities.
2. PRODUCT DEVELOPMENT (New Products + Existing Markets) — Create new products for existing customers. Moderate risk. Example: Apple creating the iPad for existing iPhone/Mac users.
3. MARKET DEVELOPMENT (Existing Products + New Markets) — Take existing products to new segments or geographies. Moderate risk. Example: IKEA entering a new country with the same product range.
4. DIVERSIFICATION (New Products + New Markets) — Highest risk. Can be related (adjacent industry) or unrelated. Example: Amazon launching AWS — cloud computing for enterprises, completely different from e-commerce.

WORKED EXAMPLE — Lululemon's Growth Strategy:
- Market Penetration: More stores in existing women's yoga market → Saturating, but low risk
- Product Development: Men's line for existing health-conscious customers → STRONG SUCCESS — grew to $2.5B
- Market Development: International expansion, especially China → WORKING — China fastest growing market
- Diversification: Acquire MIRROR (connected fitness hardware) → FAILED — $443M write-down
Key Insight: Successful turnaround came from moderate-risk moves (Product Dev + Market Dev) building on existing strengths. The one Diversification bet was the biggest failure.

APPLYING TO NONPROFITS:
- Market Penetration: Serve more clients in existing program areas with existing funders
- Product Development: Launch new programs for existing client populations
- Market Development: Take existing programs to a new geography or demographic
- Diversification: Launch an entirely new program type for a new population — highest risk, requires new capabilities

RULE OF THUMB: Exhaust Market Penetration and Product Development before attempting Market Development. Only pursue Diversification when you have strong evidence it can succeed AND existing markets are truly saturated.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Jumping to Diversification without exhausting lower-risk options first
- Treating Market Development as low risk when the new market has fundamentally different dynamics
- Confusing a product line extension (new color = Market Penetration) with Product Development (new category)



## McKinsey 7S Framework

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: McKinsey 7S Framework (McKinsey & Company, 1980s)

PURPOSE: Analyze organizational effectiveness by examining seven interconnected elements. Use when strategy is clear but execution is failing.

THE SEVEN ELEMENTS:

HARD ELEMENTS (easier to identify and manage):
1. Strategy — The plan for building and maintaining competitive advantage
2. Structure — How the organization is organized (hierarchy, reporting lines, divisions)
3. Systems — Daily processes and procedures (IT systems, performance management, budgeting)

SOFT ELEMENTS (harder to identify, often more important):
4. Shared Values — Core values and culture that guide behavior. AT THE CENTER OF THE MODEL — all other elements connect to this.
5. Style — Leadership behavior and organizational culture
6. Staff — The people, their capabilities, and how they're developed
7. Skills — The distinctive competencies of the organization

KEY PRINCIPLE: All seven elements are interconnected. You cannot change one without affecting the others. A new strategy (hard) that doesn't align with Shared Values (soft) will fail in execution.

WORKED EXAMPLE — UPS IPO Culture Preservation:
- Strategy: Changed from patient long-term investment to quarterly reporting pressure
- Structure: Added dual-class shares to preserve employee control initially
- Systems: Added public company reporting systems alongside operational systems
- Shared Values: "We all win together" — preserved for a decade, then eroded under market pressure
- Style: "Promote from within" — maintained for years, then outside CEOs brought in
- Staff: Stock awards continued but employee ownership diluted over 25 years
Conclusion: UPS preserved soft elements structurally for ~10 years post-IPO, but public market pressure on hard elements (Strategy, Systems) gradually pulled soft elements along. You can protect culture structurally for a while, but not forever.

WHEN TO USE IT OVER OTHER FRAMEWORKS:
Use 7S when the question is "why isn't this working?" rather than "what should we do?" It's a diagnostic framework for execution failures, not a strategy planning tool.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Using it when the problem is strategy, not execution (Porter's Five Forces and SWOT are better for strategy)
- Analyzing each element in isolation without identifying the misalignments between them
- Treating hard elements as more important because they're easier to see



## Profitability Framework

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: Profitability Framework (Standard Consulting Interview and Practice Tool)

PURPOSE: Diagnose why a company's profits are declining or insufficient. The standard framework consultants use before recommending any financial intervention.

THE FRAMEWORK:
Profit = Revenue - Costs

Revenue = Volume × Price
  Volume = Market Size × Market Share
  Price = function of willingness-to-pay, competition, positioning

Costs = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs
  Fixed: rent, salaries, insurance, depreciation (don't change with volume)
  Variable: materials, per-unit labor, shipping (scale with volume)

HOW TO APPLY — DRILL DOWN SYSTEMATICALLY:
1. Is revenue the problem, costs the problem, or both?
2. If revenue: is it a volume problem (fewer customers/transactions) or a price problem (lower revenue per transaction)?
3. If volume: is the market shrinking or are we losing market share?
4. If price: are we discounting, or is mix shifting toward lower-priced products?
5. If costs: is it fixed costs (too much overhead) or variable costs (inefficient operations)?
6. Always ask: is this a one-time issue or a structural trend?

WORKED EXAMPLE — Domino's Pizza (2009):
Symptom: Stock at $3, declining same-store sales
Revenue analysis: U.S. pizza market growing ~3% annually — not a market problem. Domino's was losing share to fast-casual and premium competitors — share problem. Average ticket was competitive — not a pricing problem. Root cause: VOLUME decline from MARKET SHARE LOSS caused by inferior product quality.
Cost analysis: Fixed and variable costs were normal for the franchise model. Not primarily a cost problem.
Diagnosis: Revenue problem → volume decline → market share loss → product quality
Correct Intervention: Fix the product (complete reformulation), then fix perception ("our pizza sucks" campaign). Cost-cutting would have been the WRONG answer.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Jumping to solutions before completing the diagnosis tree
- Assuming every profit problem is a cost problem (often it's revenue — confirm before assuming)
- Not breaking volume into market size vs. market share (critical distinction — different solutions)
- Ignoring mix effects (revenue per unit can drop because product mix shifts, not because prices changed)
- Missing one-time items that inflate or deflate a single period



## Customer Segmentation and Targeting (STP)

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: Customer Segmentation and Targeting — STP Process

PURPOSE: Divide a market into distinct groups with different needs, choose which segments to serve, and position your offering specifically for the target.

SEGMENTATION VARIABLES:
- Demographic: Age, income, education, occupation, family size
- Geographic: Urban/suburban/rural, region, density
- Psychographic: Values, lifestyle, personality, attitudes
- Behavioral: Usage frequency, purchase occasion, loyalty status, price sensitivity, benefit sought

THE STP PROCESS:
1. SEGMENT — Divide the market into meaningful groups based on the variables above
2. TARGET — Choose the most attractive segments based on: size, growth rate, profitability, competitive intensity, fit with your capabilities
3. POSITION — Design and communicate your offering to appeal specifically to the target segment

SEGMENT ATTRACTIVENESS SCORING:
Rate each potential segment on: Size (how many?) × Growth (is it growing?) × Accessibility (can you reach them?) × Fit (do you have the capability to serve them well?) × Competition (are others already serving them?)

WORKED EXAMPLE — Lakeside Community Theatre:
Segment 1 — Loyal Subscribers (65+): High current penetration, but declining 12%/year through attrition. Strategy: Retain, don't over-invest.
Segment 2 — Cultural Families (35-55): ~5,000 in market, low current penetration, high value (multiple tickets + concessions). Strategy: TARGET — Flex Pass, family programming.
Segment 3 — Young Professionals (25-35): ~8,000 in market, very low penetration. Strategy: Next Gen membership, late-night programming.
Segment 4 — Students (18-24): Near zero penetration, low value now but high future value. Strategy: $10 rush tickets, university co-productions.
Segment 5 — Corporate Clients: ~200 companies, near zero penetration, very high value (events, sponsorships, venue rental). Strategy: TARGET — Venue rental program, dinner-and-show packages.
Key Insight: The theater was optimizing for Segment 1 (shrinking 12%/year) while ignoring the highest-growth opportunities in Segments 2 and 5.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Trying to serve all segments equally — you'll serve none of them well
- Over-relying on demographic segmentation when behavioral segmentation is more predictive
- Ignoring segment profitability in favor of segment size
- Failing to revisit segmentation as markets evolve



## Pre-Mortem Analysis

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK: Pre-Mortem Analysis (Gary Klein, psychologist)

PURPOSE: Identify risks BEFORE they materialize by imagining that the strategy has already failed and asking why. Unlike a post-mortem (after failure), a pre-mortem is proactive.

HOW TO APPLY:
1. The team has agreed on a recommendation or strategy
2. Before finalizing, everyone assumes the strategy has been implemented and has FAILED SPECTACULARLY
3. Each person independently writes down the most plausible reasons for the failure
4. Share and discuss — look for risks that were previously unspoken or underweighted
5. Revise the strategy to mitigate the top risks identified

WHY IT WORKS:
- Overcomes groupthink — gives explicit permission to be negative about a plan everyone has been building together
- Surfaces "elephant in the room" concerns that people are reluctant to raise
- Moves from "what could go wrong?" (abstract, easy to dismiss) to "what DID go wrong?" (concrete, harder to ignore)
- Research shows pre-mortems improve prediction accuracy by 30%

WORKED EXAMPLE — YMCA Corporate Membership Program Pre-Mortem:
Assume it's 12 months from now. The program launched and FAILED. Why?

Failure 1: "We couldn't sell it — we don't have a sales team and the team lead is already overwhelmed."
→ Mitigation: Hire part-time Business Dev Coordinator BEFORE launch, not after.

Failure 2: "Companies signed up but employees didn't actually come. Usage was 15% of enrolled."
→ Mitigation: Include usage reporting for HR managers, create engagement campaigns, offer on-site programming.

Failure 3: "The pool broke down for 2 months and our key differentiator disappeared."
→ Mitigation: Accelerate pool maintenance BEFORE corporate launch; develop backup value proposition.

Failure 4: "GoodLife launched a corporate product at $30/employee/month and undercut us."
→ Mitigation: Emphasize what GoodLife can't offer (pool, childcare, community). Don't compete on price.

Failure 5: "We signed 3 companies in 6 months instead of 10. Revenue didn't cover the new hire."
→ Mitigation: Get letters of intent from interested companies BEFORE hiring. Start with contract sales help, not a full-time role.

FOR THE CONSULTING SWARM — INSTRUCTIONS:
The risk analyst and devil's advocate agents should run a pre-mortem on the consensus recommendations during Round 3. Output should include a "pre-mortem risk register" with the 3-5 most plausible failure modes and corresponding mitigations. This should appear in the final report as the Risk Assessment section.

COMMON MISTAKES:
- Treating it as optional — it should be standard for every major recommendation
- Running it with the group rather than having individuals write independently first (defeats the purpose)
- Identifying risks without proposing concrete mitigations



## Framework Selection Guide

CONSULTING FRAMEWORK SELECTION GUIDE — When to Use What

For consulting swarm agents: when analyzing a case, use 2-3 frameworks that address DIFFERENT dimensions of the problem. Do not default to one framework. A financial analyst agent should lead with the Profitability Framework, a strategy agent with Porter's Five Forces, an operations agent with Value Chain Analysis.

SITUATION → BEST FRAMEWORK(S):

Entering a new market → Porter's Five Forces + Customer Segmentation (STP)
Declining profits → Profitability Framework first, then Value Chain to find where costs/value are leaking
Growth strategy planning → Ansoff Matrix + BCG Matrix (if multiple products/programs)
Organizational change or transformation → McKinsey 7S
Product design or innovation → Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)
Quick situational overview → SWOT (always include SO/WO/ST/WT cross-reference, not just the four lists)
Risk assessment → Pre-Mortem Analysis
Evaluating a portfolio of programs or business units → BCG Growth-Share Matrix
Understanding competitive advantage → Value Chain + Porter's Five Forces
Why execution is failing despite a good strategy → McKinsey 7S + Value Chain
Nonprofit resource allocation → BCG Matrix (programs as portfolio) + Customer Segmentation
IPO or valuation → Profitability Framework + Porter's Five Forces + SWOT
Crisis response → SWOT (immediate) + Pre-Mortem (before implementing response)
Brand repositioning → Jobs to Be Done + Customer Segmentation (who are we repositioning for?)

FRAMEWORK COMBINATIONS THAT WORK WELL TOGETHER:
- Porter's Five Forces (industry level) + Value Chain (company level) = full competitive picture
- SWOT (what's the situation) + Ansoff Matrix (what should we do about it) = situation to strategy
- Profitability Framework (financial diagnosis) + McKinsey 7S (execution diagnosis) = complete turnaround analysis
- BCG Matrix (what to invest in) + Pre-Mortem (what could go wrong) = investment decision with risk

KEY PRINCIPLE: The framework is a tool, not the answer. The goal is to structure thinking, surface the right questions, and communicate clearly — not to fill in boxes. A well-applied framework leads to 2-3 sharp insights. A box-filling exercise produces a deck no one remembers.
